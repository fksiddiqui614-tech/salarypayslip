How Internal Auditor Salary Is Reported

Internal Auditor is not published as a separate national wage category in the current BLS occupational wage tables. O*NET lists Internal Auditor as a reported job title within Accountants and Auditors, SOC 13-2011.00. The May 2025 national median for this broader occupation was $83,680 per year, with a median hourly wage of $40.23.

The occupational benchmark includes accountants and several auditing roles, so it should not be interpreted as a guaranteed salary for every Internal Auditor. Actual compensation can differ according to audit specialization, employer, experience, credentials and location.

Internal Audit Roles & Specializations

Internal auditing covers more than financial statement review. Depending on the organization, Internal Auditors may examine operational processes, technology systems, regulatory compliance, cybersecurity controls, enterprise risks and fraud-related activities.

Internal Audit Specialty Typical Focus
Financial Internal Audit Reviews financial processes, records, reconciliations and reporting controls.
Operational Audit Evaluates business processes, efficiency and operational controls.
Compliance Audit Examines adherence to laws, regulations, policies and internal requirements.
IT Audit Reviews information systems, technology controls, access and data processes.
Risk Audit Evaluates enterprise risks and the effectiveness of risk-management controls.
Fraud & Investigative Audit Examines unusual activity, control weaknesses and potential financial misconduct.

What Does an Internal Auditor Do?

Internal Auditors evaluate how an organization manages financial, operational and compliance risks. Their work often involves reviewing processes, testing controls, collecting evidence and communicating findings to management or an audit committee.

  • Evaluate internal controls
  • Perform risk assessments
  • Test financial and operational processes
  • Review policies and procedures
  • Investigate control weaknesses
  • Document audit evidence and findings
  • Prepare internal audit reports
  • Recommend improvements to controls and processes
  • Monitor remediation of previously identified issues
  • Communicate significant risks to management

Technology is also increasingly important in internal audit as organizations use enterprise software, cloud systems, automated controls and large datasets across their operations.

Education & Credentials for Internal Auditors

A bachelor's degree in accounting, finance, business or a related field is a common educational starting point for Internal Auditor positions. Employers may also value coursework or experience in risk management, information systems, compliance and data analysis.

Common Educational Background

  • Bachelor's degree in Accounting
  • Bachelor's degree in Finance
  • Bachelor's degree in Business Administration
  • Accounting Information Systems
  • Master's degree in Accounting, Finance or Business

Professional Credentials

  • Certified Internal Auditor (CIA)
  • Certified Public Accountant (CPA)
  • Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA)
  • Certified Fraud Examiner (CFE)
  • Certified Management Accountant (CMA)

The most useful credential depends on the type of Internal Audit work. Technology-focused positions may value information-systems expertise, while financial and assurance roles may place greater emphasis on accounting qualifications.

Future of Internal Auditing in the USA

Internal Audit work is evolving as organizations adopt cloud computing, artificial intelligence, automated workflows, enterprise software and larger digital datasets. These technologies can change how Auditors collect evidence, test controls and monitor risks.

Routine review activities may become increasingly automated, while Internal Auditors may spend more time interpreting data, evaluating emerging risks, reviewing technology controls and advising management on process improvements.

Areas Increasingly Important for Internal Auditors

  • Risk analytics
  • Continuous control monitoring
  • Artificial intelligence governance
  • Cybersecurity controls
  • Cloud technology audits
  • Data analytics
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Enterprise risk management
  • Fraud detection and prevention
  • Technology and access controls

The broader federal outlook indicates that technological change is expected to make accounting and auditing work more analytical and advisory as routine activities become increasingly automated.
